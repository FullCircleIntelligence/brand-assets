# Course B — Disability, Reasonable Accommodations & Modifications (Vermont)

*Also branded internally as "Fair Housing II."*

**Prerequisite:** Course A
**Audience:** All client-facing staff, maintenance, and accounting (tracked)

**Cadence:** Annual, with an out-of-cycle re-issue whenever the Watch List in

## Module 1 — Disability Under Fair Housing Law (15 min) · full track, `MT` `AC` abbreviated

**Objective:** Learners understand how broad the definition is and stop making
eligibility judgments.

1.1 The three-part definition: a physical or mental impairment that substantially
limits one or more major life activities; a record of such an impairment; or being
regarded as having such an impairment.
1.2 What is included and routinely missed: mental health conditions, PTSD,
substance use disorder in recovery, HIV status, chronic illness, learning
disabilities, intellectual and developmental disabilities, chronic pain,
autoimmune conditions, sensory disabilities, and episodic conditions that are
disabling when active.
1.3 What is excluded: current illegal use of a controlled substance, and
individuals who pose a direct threat under an individualized assessment.
1.4 **Non-obvious and non-apparent disabilities.** Most requests you will receive
are for disabilities you cannot see. Staff must not use visibility as a
credibility test.
1.5 You do not get to decide whether someone "is really disabled." You verify only
what the law lets you verify, and only when the disability and need are not
already known or apparent.
1.6 Vermont note: 9 V.S.A. § 4503(a)(9) expressly prohibits discrimination
because a person relies on aids such as attendants, specially trained animals,
wheelchairs, or similar appliances or devices.
1.7 Marijuana: lawful under Vermont law, still a federally controlled substance —
flag the tension and route rather than deciding, particularly at federally
assisted properties.

## Module 2 — Accommodation vs. Modification: Get the Category Right (10 min) · full track

**Objective:** Learners can classify a request correctly, because the category
determines who pays.

| | Reasonable **Accommodation** | Reasonable **Modification** |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | A rule, policy, practice, or service | The physical structure |
| Authority | 9 V.S.A. § 4503(a)(10); 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B) | 9 V.S.A. § 4503(a)(9); 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(A) |
| Who pays (private housing) | The **provider** | The **requester** |
| Restoration | N/A | Provider may require restoration of the interior to prior condition, reasonable wear excepted |
| Extra deposit | Prohibited | **Prohibited** under Vermont law |
| Examples | Assistance animal despite a no-pets rule; assigned accessible parking space; changed rent due date; permission for a live-in aide; transfer to a ground-floor unit; extra time to remedy a lease violation caused by a disability; accepting a third-party payer | Grab bars; a ramp; widened doorway; roll-in shower; lever handles; visual doorbell; lowered cabinets or countertops; raised garden bed |

2.1 The gray zone — reserving an existing parking space is an *accommodation*;
striping and signing a new accessible space is a *modification*. Teach the test:
does it change a rule, or does it change the building?
2.2 **Who pays flips** when the housing receives federal financial assistance —
Section 504 requires the recipient to pay for structural changes up to undue
financial and administrative burden. Identify which Full Circle properties, if
any, are federally assisted. `LEAD`
2.3 Modifications in association common elements `AM` — the owner generally bears
the cost, but the association cannot unreasonably refuse, cannot use architectural
review as a veto, and must consider the request under fair housing standards
rather than covenant standards.
2.4 New construction and substantial rehab `LEAD` `AM` — the seven design and
construction requirements under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(C) for covered multifamily
dwellings first occupied after March 13, 1991, plus Vermont Access Rules and any
applicable ADA obligations. A feature the law *required* the building to have is
not an accommodation you get to charge for or refuse.

## Module 3 — Intake: Recognizing and Receiving a Request (15 min) · ALL TRACKS

**Objective:** No request is missed, mis-timed, or answered by the wrong person.

3.1 There is no magic language. A request need not be written, need not be on your
form, need not say "accommodation," and need not name a diagnosis.
3.2 Requests arrive through every channel: maintenance tickets, voicemails, texts,
an offhand comment during a showing, a note from a caseworker, a comment in an
association meeting, a line in a response to a lease violation notice.
3.3 **A request made in response to enforcement is still a request.** When a
resident answers a nuisance notice or a pet violation with "I have a disability
and this animal helps me," the enforcement action pauses and the accommodation
process starts. Missing this is one of the most damaging errors staff make.
3.4 Who may make one: the individual, a household member, or anyone acting on
their behalf.
3.5 Logging: date received, channel, who received it, what was asked for, and the
clock starting.
3.6 Your acknowledgment script — what to say and what never to say.
3.7 **Response timeliness.** Set an internal standard (e.g. acknowledge within 2
business days, decide within 10 business days of receiving sufficient
information). Unreasonable delay is treated as denial.
3.8 Accessible request channels: you cannot require the request in a format the
person's disability makes difficult.
3.9 Language access at intake.

## Module 4 — The Interactive Process (20 min) · full track

**Objective:** Learners can run a documented, good-faith dialogue instead of
issuing a flat yes or no.

4.1 What the interactive process is: a prompt, good-faith, two-way conversation
to understand the need and find a workable answer.
4.2 The two questions that govern everything:
  1. Does the requester have a disability under the definition?
  2. Is there an **identifiable relationship (nexus)** between the disability and
     the requested accommodation — will it actually help?
4.3 **Alternatives.** If the specific request is unreasonable, you must explore
whether an alternative meets the need. You may propose an alternative; you may
not simply substitute your preference for an effective accommodation. Silence and
a denial letter are not an interactive process.
4.4 Documentation of the dialogue — dated notes of every contact, offer, and
response.
4.5 Common failure modes: waiting for the perfect form, requiring the request in
writing, going silent, letting the request sit in a shared inbox, treating the
architectural review calendar as the timeline `AM`, or telling the resident to
"take it up with the owner."
4.6 What to do when the resident stops responding — document your outreach, and
what closing the file requires.

## Module 5 — Verification: What You May and May Not Ask (20 min) · full track

**Objective:** Learners request the minimum necessary, from the right source, and
never touch a diagnosis.

5.1 **When you may request verification** — only when the disability or the
disability-related need is **not known or readily apparent**. If it is obvious,
asking anyway is itself a problem.
5.2 **What you may ask for:** confirmation that the person has a disability as
defined, and that there is a disability-related need for the requested
accommodation.
5.3 **What you may NOT ask for:** the diagnosis, the nature or severity of the
condition, medical records, treatment history, medications, or an in-person
examination. You may not require a specific provider, a specific form, an annual
re-verification of a permanent condition, or a fee.
5.4 **Who may verify:** a reliable third party in a position to know — a
physician, but also a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, psychologist,
social worker, therapist, peer support specialist, case manager, or another
reliable source. A telehealth relationship is not automatically unreliable, but
you may reasonably question a certificate purchased online with no
practitioner relationship. Teach the distinction carefully — over-policing
documentation is a bigger source of liability than under-policing it.
5.5 **Assistance animals — the Vermont-first rule.** `ALL TRACKS`
  - Federal position has moved: HUD withdrew FHEO Notice 2020-01 (January 28,
    2020) and FHEO Notice 2013-01 (April 25, 2013) on September 17, 2025.
  - **Vermont law did not change.** Vermont prohibits refusing to permit an
    assistance animal at a dwelling *whether or not the animal has received
    special training*, subject to individualized defenses: the specific animal
    poses a direct threat to health or safety, would cause substantial physical
    damage, would substantially interfere with others' reasonable enjoyment, or
    would not constitute a reasonable accommodation. Assistance animals include
    animals that provide emotional support that ameliorates or mitigates
    identified symptoms or effects of a disability.
  - Operational rules: no pet fee, no pet rent, no extra deposit, no breed or
    weight limits, no "no pets" defense, no vest or certification requirement, no
    registry requirement. The resident remains responsible for actual damage and
    for the animal's behavior.
  - The defenses are **individualized** — about *this* animal's actual conduct,
    documented. Never about the species or breed in the abstract.
  - Multiple animals, unusual species, and outdoor animals: evaluate, document,
    escalate — do not reflex-deny.
  - Service animals under the ADA in public-facing spaces (a rental office, some
    association facilities) follow a narrower ADA framework — the two permitted
    questions. Teach the boundary so staff do not apply the ADA test to a housing
    request. `LS` `AM`
5.6 **Confidentiality.** Disability information is kept in a separate,
access-restricted file, not in the general resident or owner file, not in the
ledger, and not in shared drives. Need-to-know disclosure only. Never discussed
with other residents, board members, or vendors. Board members in particular do
not get the documentation — they get the decision. `AM`
5.7 Storage, retention, and destruction of verification documents.

## Module 6 — Deciding: Reasonableness, Undue Burden, Fundamental Alteration (20 min) · full track, `LEAD`

**Objective:** Decision-makers apply the correct legal standards and can defend
the analysis in writing.

6.1 The default is **yes**. Denial is the exception and carries the burden.
6.2 The three grounds for denial, each requiring an individualized, documented
analysis:
  - **Undue financial and administrative burden** — assessed against the
    provider's overall resources, not a single property's monthly budget; must be
    quantified, not asserted
  - **Fundamental alteration** of the provider's operations or program
  - **Direct threat** to the health or safety of others, or substantial physical
    damage to property, that cannot be eliminated or reduced by another
    accommodation — based on current, objective evidence about *this* individual,
    never on stereotype, fear, or generalization about a condition
6.3 What is **not** a valid reason to deny: cost inconvenience, other residents'
objections, covenant language, "we've never done that," insurance company
preference, precedent-setting fears, an ARC meeting schedule, or "the owner said
no." `AM`
6.4 **Owner instructions.** When a property owner directs you to deny a lawful
accommodation, you have a fair housing problem, not an owner-relations problem.
Teach the escalation path explicitly — this is a real and recurring scenario in
third-party management. `RPM` `AM` `LEAD`
6.5 If the specific request is unreasonable, you must still consider an
alternative that meets the need before denying.
6.6 **The written decision letter.** Required elements: what was requested, what
was decided, the specific reason for any denial, the alternatives considered and
offered, the interactive process history, the internal appeal or reconsideration
path, and contact information for the Vermont Human Rights Commission and CVOEO.
Template-driven, reviewed before it goes out.
6.7 Conditional approvals and reasonable conditions — what you may and may not
attach.
6.8 Implementation and follow-through — an approved accommodation that is never
actually delivered is a denial. Track to completion.
6.9 Re-evaluation, changed circumstances, and revocation — narrow, documented
grounds only.
6.10 Escalation and legal review triggers: any denial, any direct-threat analysis,
any structural modification in a common element, any request touching a federally
assisted property, any request that arrives with an attorney or advocate attached.

## Module 7 — Accessibility Beyond Individual Requests (15 min) · `LEAD` `AM` `RPM` `MT`

7.1 The seven design and construction requirements for covered multifamily
dwellings first occupied after March 13, 1991:
  1. Accessible building entrance on an accessible route
  2. Accessible and usable public and common use areas
  3. Doors usable by a person in a wheelchair
  4. Accessible route into and through the dwelling unit
  5. Light switches, outlets, thermostats, and environmental controls in
     accessible locations
  6. Reinforced bathroom walls for later grab bar installation
  7. Usable kitchens and bathrooms
7.2 Coverage: all units in buildings with an elevator and four or more units; all
ground-floor units in buildings with four or more units and no elevator.
7.3 Common areas open to persons beyond owners, residents, and guests — rental and
sales offices, some pools and clubhouses — may also trigger the ADA. `LS` `AM`
7.4 Section 504 and 24 CFR Part 8 if federally assisted: 5% of units (or at least
one) accessible for mobility disabilities, 2% for hearing/vision, plus program
accessibility and effective communication obligations.
7.5 Vermont Access Rules for public buildings and the Vermont Fire and Building
Safety Code — coordinate with, do not substitute for, federal requirements.
7.6 **Maintaining what exists** `MT` — accessible parking kept striped, signed,
and unobstructed; accessible routes kept clear of snow, bins, landscaping, and
stored materials (a Vermont-specific and genuinely important operational point);
automatic door openers kept working; visual alarms tested; grab bars never
removed during a turn.
7.7 Effective communication and auxiliary aids — accessible notices, meeting
materials, interpreters for association meetings, accessible online portals.
7.8 Emergency planning and evacuation for residents with disabilities.

## Module 8 — Role Tracks (variable)

### 8A Leasing track (~15 min) `LS`
Handling a request at inquiry or application. Never denying an application because
of a pending accommodation request. Accessible unit inventory and not renting an
accessible unit out from under a person who needs it. Showing accessible features
accurately. The two-question ADA service animal rule in the office versus the
housing standard. Answering "do you allow pets?" without triggering a violation.

### 8B Association track (~20 min) `AM`
Routing an ARC application that is actually a modification request. Assistance
animals versus pet covenants and weight limits. Assigned and accessible parking in
common elements. Board education and the board's exposure. Confidentiality from
the board. Ramps and lifts on limited common elements. Accessible meetings.
Handling an owner-against-owner complaint. Documenting selective enforcement
defenses. Fair housing law overrides inconsistent covenants.

### 8C Maintenance track (~60 min total: Modules 1 abbrev., 3, 5.5, 7.6 + this)
Recognizing a request in a work order. Language to use and language never to use.
Never removing or blocking an accessibility feature. Working around an assistance
animal. Entry and privacy. Reporting hazards to accessible routes. Snow and ice
management on accessible routes and at accessible parking. Documenting work
neutrally. Escalating same day. Not making promises about what the company will do.

### 8D Accounting / finance track (~45 min: Modules 1 abbrev., 3, 5.6 + this) `AC`
No pet fee, pet rent, or extra deposit for an assistance animal, and no extra
deposit for a modification. Rent due date changes and late fee waivers tied to
benefit deposit dates as accommodations, not favors. Accepting third-party and
representative-payee payments. Voucher portions and the income multiplier trap.
Payment plans as accommodations. Ledger coding hygiene. What to do when medical
documentation lands in your inbox. Collections and eviction referrals while a
request is pending — stop and escalate.

## Module 9 — Practice (15 min) · full track

A scenario bank with branching decisions, drawn from the role-specific bank in
`04-role-matrix.md`. Minimum eight scenarios, of which at least three are Vermont-
distinct (assistance animal without training or certification; a voucher holder's
accommodation request; an immigration-status/no-SSN applicant who also requests an
accommodation).

## Assessment and completion requirements

- 25-question scenario assessment; 85% to pass (higher than Course A — this is a
  procedural course); two retakes.
- At least one free-text or structured exercise in which the learner drafts or
  critiques a decision letter.
- Mandatory 100% on a short gate quiz covering: never ask for a diagnosis; never
  charge for an assistance animal; delay is denial; never deny on your own
  authority if you are not a designated decision-maker.
- Job aids issued at completion: intake form, verification request template,
  decision letter template, one-page "what you may and may not ask" card.
